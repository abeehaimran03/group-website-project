Chinese Foods

Breakfast:

There is a wide variety of breakfast food in China.

  • The most common meal would be deep-fried dough sticks (yóutiáo) served with soya milk. (dòujiāng) Deep-fried dough sticks are self-explanatory. They are made of flour, knead into dough, and fried in oil until they are crispy and golden in colour. Soya milk is made from soya beans after being blended in the mixer. Often, Chinese people would dip yóutiáo into their soya milk as this gives the yóutiáo a sweet taste and slightly softer texture.
  • Another popular food eaten for breakfast would be steamed buns (bāozi). There are 2 types:
    1. Salted steamed buns,
    2. Sweet steamed buns.

    The salty ones are usually filled with meat or vegetables like ground pork, beef, or chives etc. Sweet steamed buns may be filled with red bean paste, or creamy custard.

Lunch:

  • Certainly, you would expect a Chinese person to consume rice or noodles for lunch.
  • Other than the above they also eat a dish called “Hot Pot for One” (mào cài). This is simply where the person chooses the ingredients they'd like e.g. tofu, meat products, mushrooms etc., and put them in a bamboo basket, and cook them in a spicy broth.

Dinner:

  • Hot Pot is a popular choice for dinner. Chinese people gather and cook their meats and vegetables in a simmering pot that's placed in front of them. The soup base differs depending on the group's taste. It can either be spicy or very mild.
  • Peking Duck is another dish that'll appear on the dinner table quite often. It originated from Beijing, and it features small pancakes, some vegetables, and sauce. The duck's skin would be crispy, and a slice would be cut off to be wrapped in a small pancake with some vegetables and sauce.

Desserts:

  • Dim sum (morning tea) isn't a dish, albeit its name is associated with having breakfast. The dishes, e.g. buns, shrimp dumplings, pastries, or steamed vermicelli rolls, are served in steamer baskets or sometimes on small plates and are accompanied by tea.
  • Glutinous rice balls (tāngyuán) are akin to mochis. The texture is chewy, and they contain sweet fillings e.g. peanut, or black sesame. They are best served hot in a sweet broth (which is usually merely hot water with sugar added to flavour the soup).

Street food:

  • Stinky tofu (chòu dòufu) is a very controversial street food. Some Chinese people love them while some can't stand the smell. It is a fermented tofu that has a crispy, black skin with a strong odour.
  • Tanghulu (bīng táng húlú) doesn't have a translation unfortunately. It is fruits, e.g. strawberries or traditionally Chinese hawthorn, being fixated in a row on a bamboo stick. They are then coated with caramelised sugar and left to cool. This forms a crispy sugar coat covering the fruit.
